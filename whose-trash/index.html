The global picture

Scientists led by Carmen Morales-Caselles pooled samples of more than 12 million pieces of litter from 7 major river and ocean environments around the world.

80% was plastic, 7% metal, 5% glass, and 3% fabric.

Four takeout items (single-use bags, bottles, food containers and wrappers) made up 44% of the trash counted.

The study sorted litter into 112 item types, but the top 10 alone make up around 75% of all the litter.

Top 10 items

Whose trash is it?

Here are the top 10 items floating off the world's high-income coasts, like the U.S., Canada, United Kingdom, etc. Each item is sized by its share of all the litter found there.

Hover over any piece to see what it is.

The largest percentage of litter was fishing gear. 43.5% of the litter is fishing items, weights and rope. "Fishing related" items alone are 31.7%, the greatest number in this dataset.

Takeout trash took up 17.4% of the litter. Bags, bottles, food containers and wrappers together are smaller than the fishing items alone.

In East Asia & the Pacific. The pile flips to 47.5% takeout. Wrappers alone are 18.2%.

In Latin America & the Caribbean, plastic bags are most frequently found at 20.1%. Takeout totals 43.7%.

In North Africa & the Middle East, food containers lead at 17.1%. Takeout totals 41.9%.

The global "44% takeout" headline is true on average, but not true everywhere, especially on the richest coasts.

    So whose trash is it?

    On high-income coasts like the U.S., the litter in the water is mostly fishing gear. Almost everywhere else, it is mostly the packaging from food and drinks bought on the go.

    About the data

    • The dataset lists only each region's top 10 items, so each column does not add up to 100%.
    • Shares are of all litter counted in nearshore waters, within 100 km of the coast.
    • "High income" is an income group, not a place. This is how the World Bank groups countries.
    • The data was published in 2021. The study included samples of more than 12 million litter items from 7 major river and ocean environments.
    • In the piles, each item's area is proportional to its share.
    • Takeout: plastic bags, plastic bottles, food containers and wrappers. Fishing gear: fishing-related items, nets, weights and rope.
